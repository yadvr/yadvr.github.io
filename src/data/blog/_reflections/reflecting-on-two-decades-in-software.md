After 19 years, I think I've written my last line of code by hand.

I started in 2007 at IIT-BHU, learning C/C++, Assembly, Python and Linux. Out of
university, I never imagined I'd spend 14 years in Java and infrastructure on
Apache CloudStack, eventually becoming its top contributor by commits. Over time
my role shifted from writing code to writing specs for stakeholders and the
engineering team I led. Both the hands-on work and the leadership taught me a
lot.

Then AI changed things quickly. Last year I went from prompting for snippets, to
AI-native development with specs and feedback loops, to multi-agent systems.
Today it looks more like running an AI software factory: I define a blueprint
with the intent, constraints and tests, and agents build, check and iterate.
I've even started giving my agents their own VMs and hosts to do end-to-end
development and QA.

Coming to terms with that has been humbling. For most of my career, learning a
new language, framework or paradigm was how I stayed sharp. That's no longer
where my leverage is. It's in knowing what to build, specifying it precisely,
and being able to tell whether the result is actually right.

One thing I didn't expect: most of my new work is in strongly typed, compiled
languages, mainly Rust, plus Go and Swift depending on the use case. Not because
I write it, but because a strict compiler is a great partner for agents. It
catches mistakes before anything ships.

Which makes me wonder where this goes. Will agents keep relying on compilers,
type systems and OS abstractions as guardrails? Or will they eventually skip all
that and work in some AI-native representation, maybe straight to machine code,
that we haven't invented yet?

Curious how other engineering leaders are seeing their own role change.
